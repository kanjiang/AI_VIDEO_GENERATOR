# Methodology

Three books underpin the work at all times.

---

## Robert McKee — Story

### Controlling Idea
One sentence in the form: "Life becomes X when the protagonist does Y because Z." That is the film's controlling idea. Every scene must serve it.

If you cannot express the controlling idea in one sentence, the film has no spine. Start with the idea, then write the scenes.

### Scene Value Shift
In every scene, **something changes** from positive to negative or from negative to positive. A value enters the scene in one state and leaves it in another.

**A scene without a value turn is not a scene.** It is only description.

Before writing a scene, answer:
- What value enters the scene? For example: trust.
- In what state does it enter? For example: the team trusts the leader.
- In what state does it leave? For example: the team suspects the leader.
- How does that happen? What action flips the value?

Common value pairs:
- Control ↔ helplessness
- Trust ↔ betrayal
- Love ↔ obsession
- Life ↔ death
- Freedom ↔ captivity
- Discipline ↔ chaos
- Team ↔ tool
- Hope ↔ despair
- Knowledge ↔ ignorance

### Inciting Incident
The event that knocks the protagonist's life out of balance. It must happen **within the first quarter** of the film. In a 90-minute film, that means before roughly minute 22.

Without that event, the story does not begin. The protagonist's life before it and after it are two different worlds.

### Progressive Complications
Each new conflict must be stronger than the one before it. The gap between what the protagonist expects and what the action produces must grow.

If the third-act conflict is weaker than the first-act conflict, the structure is broken.

### Crisis → Climax → Resolution
- **Crisis:** the protagonist's final dilemma, a choice between two incompatible values.
- **Climax:** the action that answers the dilemma, the most physical and forceful point of the film.
- **Resolution:** the brief coordinate of the new world after the climax.

### The Plus / Minus Pendulum
The protagonist cannot keep winning forever or keep losing forever. The story must alternate, and the amplitude grows each time.

In a falling arc, the positives become local and temporary while the negatives become systemic and large. In a rising arc, the reverse happens.

### Show, Don't Tell
Replace internal monologue with actions and objects. An object in the frame says more than a line of dialogue.

A pregnancy test instead of the line "She's pregnant."
A torn letter instead of "He left her."
An empty chair instead of "He's alone."

## Joseph Campbell — The Hero with a Thousand Faces

Use Campbell **as a diagnostic tool, not as dogma**. Not every story is a pure hero's journey, especially anti-hero stories or tragedies. In those cases, a mirrored descent, a *katabasis* without return, may fit better.

### The 12 Stages

1. **Ordinary World** — the protagonist is in equilibrium. Show what exists so the audience understands what may be lost.
2. **Call to Adventure** — an event demands that the protagonist leave the known world.
3. **Refusal of the Call** — the protagonist hesitates or fears the path ahead. Without this, the character becomes a function rather than a person.
4. **Meeting the Mentor** — someone offers a tool, skill, or knowledge. This can also be a false mentor who is actually a shadow.
5. **Crossing the Threshold** — the protagonist crosses the border between worlds.
6. **Tests, Allies, Enemies** — adapting to the new world.
7. **Approach to the Inmost Cave** — preparation for the central ordeal.
8. **Ordeal** — confrontation with death or with the shadow.
9. **Reward** — the protagonist gains what they came for.
10. **The Road Back** — return to the ordinary world, or in an anti-hero tragedy, the inability to return.
11. **Resurrection** — the final test. The protagonist dies symbolically and is reborn.
12. **Return with the Elixir** — the protagonist brings something new back to the world.

### Archetypes
- **Hero:** the active engine of the plot.
- **Shadow:** the anti-hero or dark mirror of the hero.
- **Mentor:** the figure who provides a tool.
- **Threshold Guardian:** the barrier at the entry to a new world.
- **Herald:** the messenger of the call.
- **Shapeshifter:** the figure who changes sides.
- **Trickster:** the comic or disruptive rule-breaker.

In an anti-heroic tragedy, **the mentor is often the shadow**. The hero does not realize the teacher is also the enemy.

## Aristotle — Poetics

### Hamartia — The Fatal Flaw
Hamartia is not a moral sin. It is a defect in character that triggers the tragedy. A strong protagonist has a trait that is a virtue in ordinary life and a catastrophe in exceptional circumstances.

"I love too fiercely" works as hamartia in a romantic tragedy.
"I trust everyone" works in a political tragedy.
"I cannot forgive" works in a family tragedy.

Hamartia must be **visible in the first scene** and **explode in the climax**.

### Peripeteia — Reversal
An action that should produce X instead produces the opposite of X.

Trying to save someone, the character kills them. Trying to protect something, the character destroys it. Trying to open a door, the character closes it forever.

A strong film usually has 2 to 3 reversals that escalate in force.

### Anagnorisis — Recognition
The moment when the hero or the audience realizes what had been hidden.

The strongest recognition often **coincides with reversal**. The character learns the truth at the same moment the action turns into its opposite.

### Catharsis
The ending should produce a mixed feeling of pity for the protagonist and fear for oneself: "I could have become that person."

### Unity of Action
Aristotle requires the tragedy to move toward a single point. Every action, object, and line must work toward the final point.

**If a scene does not serve the final point, it is unnecessary.** Simple test: remove the scene. Does the film change? If not, cut it.

## HOW TO USE THIS

Before writing each scene:
1. What is the controlling idea?
2. What value enters the scene and what value leaves it?
3. Which Campbell stage applies, if any?
4. How does the scene work on the protagonist's hamartia?
5. Is this a reversal scene or a setup scene?

If the answers are empty, the scene is either unnecessary or needs to be rethought.
