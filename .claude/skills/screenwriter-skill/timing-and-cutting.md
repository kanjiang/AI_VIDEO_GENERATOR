# Screen Time and Cutting Length

## CORE RULE

"One Hollywood-format page ≈ one minute of screen time" is an **average** rule. It works for mixed dialogue/action scripts. It does **not** apply literally to action-heavy or montage scenes.

---

## SCENE TYPES BY SPEED

### Very fast, 5 to 15 seconds

- **Montage scenes** — 5 to 10 short action lines flowing visually. On the page: half a page. On screen: 8 to 12 seconds.
- **Establishing shots** — a location image without dialogue or action. 3 to 5 seconds.
- **Match-cut transitions** — 2 to 3 seconds.

Example, about 8 to 10 seconds:

```
INT. ARMORY — DAY
The door OPENS.
The walls are lined with swords.
QUICK MONTAGE:
— A hand grabs a spear.
— A sword leaves the sheath.
— Bare feet run down the corridor.
```

That is half a page, but about 10 seconds on screen.

### Fast, 15 to 45 seconds

- **Action beats with short lines** — a chase with one line, a single fight.
- **Intercut scenes** — a short return to a parallel line.

### Medium, 45 seconds to 1:30

- **Dialogue with action** — 2 or 3 lines plus physical action.
- **Breathing scenes** — a quiet moment before or after the climax.

### Long, 1:30 to 3:00

- **Dramatic dialogue with a turn** — confrontation, confession, rupture.
- **Action sequence** — chase plus fight plus rescue.
- **Complex scene with multiple beats** — setup, conflict, decision.

### Very long, 3:00 to 7:00

- **Climax** — final confrontation with a moral choice as all lines converge.
- **Big set piece** — a staged sequence with repeated tempo shifts.

---

## HOW TO ESTIMATE SCREEN TIME

Do not estimate by pages. Estimate by beats inside each scene.

**Algorithm for one scene:**

1. **Slug line + establishing** — 3 to 5 seconds, or 0 if it is a matched cut from the previous scene.
2. **Each action line separately:**
   - Simple action, one verb and one object: 1 to 2 seconds.
   - Complex action, several movements in one line: 3 to 5 seconds.
   - Major physical event, such as an explosion, fall, or transformation: 5 to 10 seconds.
3. **Each line of dialogue:** average speech is about 150 words per minute, roughly 2.5 words per second.
   - Short line: "Go." = about 1 second.
   - Medium line: 4 to 5 seconds.
   - Long line, 10 or more words: 6 to 10 seconds.
4. **Pauses and silence** count separately. A "long pause" is at least 3 to 5 seconds.
5. **Reaction shots** without dialogue or major action: 2 to 3 seconds each.

Add it all up and you get the real screen time for the scene.

---

## RECHECK

If your calculated time strongly disagrees with your intuition, recalculate. The most common mistake is overestimating length. What feels like a long scene on the page may only play for 30 seconds on screen.

A strong check is to time a similar scene from a reference film and compare it to your written material.

---

## CUTTING TO A TARGET LENGTH

When the user wants the script to fit a target runtime:

1. **Calculate the current runtime** by scene in a table.
2. **Find the gap** between current runtime and target runtime.
3. **Sort scenes by cut priority:**
   - Easiest to cut: repetition, parallel strikes of the same action, excessive reaction shots.
   - Medium priority: long breathing scenes, extended landscape beats.
   - Do not touch: climax, reversals, recognition, key turning points.

---

## COMMON CUT POINTS, FROM SAFE TO RISKY

### Safe cuts

1. **Duplicate beats** — the same emotional beat repeats in three scenes. Keep the strongest one and delete the others.
2. **Parallel hits** — three battering-ram strikes, four repeated attempts, five crowd reactions. Reduce to two iterations.
3. **Dialogue that duplicates visible action** — if the character is crying, remove the line "I'm sad."
4. **Transition scenes without a value turn** — the hero moves from location A to B and the conversation changes nothing.

### Medium-risk cuts

5. **Overdetailed descriptions** — costumes or locations described in too much detail. Compress them into one line.
6. **Expanded actions** — the hero does three things where one would do. Keep the essential move.
7. **Too many reaction shots inside a scene** — compress four faces down to two.

### Risky cuts

8. **A full comic or emotional interlude scene.** It may slow the pace, but it also adds tone.
9. **A secondary character thread.** It can be reduced, but not removed blindly.

### Never cut these first

10. **Inciting Incident**
11. **Reversals**
12. **Recognition**
13. **Crisis dilemma**
14. **Climax**
15. **Setup objects** that pay off later

---

## THE RULE: IF YOU REMOVE IT, DOES THE FILM CHANGE?

Test every scene by removing it mentally. Does the ending of the film change?

- **Yes** → the scene is necessary.
- **No** → the scene is expendable. Cut it.
- **Only the tone changes** → it is a breathing scene. It can be cut if runtime is tight, but something will be lost.

---

## BILINGUAL SCREENPLAYS AND TIME

If the screenplay is bilingual, dialogue in English plus Russian, or the reverse, **the duplicated dialogue does not count toward screen time**. Count only the primary spoken language. The translation is for reading, not for shooting.

When estimating the length of a bilingual document, the page count may grow by 1 to 2 pages because of translation, but the real screen time stays the same as the monolingual version.
